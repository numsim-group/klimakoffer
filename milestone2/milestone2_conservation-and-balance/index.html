Conservation laws

Many processes in nature and engineering can be modeled (described) with a simple principle: the principle of conservation. Consider a closed domain Ω∈Rn\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^nΩ∈Rn and a quantity u=u(x,t)∈Ru=u(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}u=u(x,t)∈R that is defined for all x∈Ωx\in\Omegax∈Ω and t≥0t\geq 0t≥0. The function u(x,t)u(x,t)u(x,t) typically describes a physical quantity such as mass, momentum, or energy.

We are interested in modeling the temporal evolution of uuu (change in time ttt). Observations of nature of the behaviour of such quantities lead to the following simple principle:

Observation: The temporal change of u(x,t)u(x,t)u(x,t) in a sub-domain ω⊂Ω\omega\subset\Omegaω⊂Ω is equal to the amount that gets generated or destroyed inside of ω\omegaω in addition to the flux balance inwards or outwards through the surface/boundary ∂ω\partial\omega∂ω.

The quantity of uuu changes, if:

(i) There is a positive/negative source inside of the sub-domain ω\omegaω

(ii) There is a positive/negative flux balance through the boundary of the sub-domain ∂ω\partial\omega∂ω

Famous example: Bathtub

The amount of water in the bathtub (blue) changes due to the sources (green) and sinks (red).

Depending on the process we want to model, we need to chose the quantity of interest u(x,t)u(x,t)u(x,t), a model for the flux f⃗(u,x,t)\vec{f}(u,x,t)f​(u,x,t) and a model for the source term S(u,x,t)S(u,x,t)S(u,x,t). There are many examples of such models, e.g., mass and momentum conservation in fluid mechanics. We consider here as an example the so-called heat equation, or heat transfer equation.

For the heat equation we are interested in the change of temperature T(x,t)T(x,t)T(x,t) (which is strongly related to the internal energy of a body) in space and time, hence our choice for the unknown quantity is u=Tu = Tu=T. Next, we need a model for the flux. Jean Babtiste Joseph Fourier (1822) gave a model for the heat flux, where the flux of heat is negative proportional to the temperature difference (heat goes from high temperatures to lower temperatures), i.e.

For scalar PDEs with two independent variables xxx and ttt of second order (the maximum derivatives are second-order derivatives) it is common to distinguish between different types of PDEs. Assuming a scalar second order PDE of the general form

auxx+buxt+cutt+dux+eut+fu+g=0, a u_{xx} + b u_{xt} + c u_{tt} + d u_x + e u_t + f u + g = 0, auxx​+buxt​+cutt​+dux​+eut​+fu+g=0,

we can define the quantity

Δ(x,t)=a(x,t) c(x,t)−b(x,t)24 \Delta(x,t) = a(x,t)\,c(x,t) - \frac{b(x,t)^2}{4} Δ(x,t)=a(x,t)c(x,t)−4b(x,t)2​

to get a classification of the different types

(i) Δ(x,t)>0\Delta(x,t) > 0Δ(x,t)>0, the PDE is elliptic in (x,t)(x,t)(x,t)

(ii) Δ(x,t)=0\Delta(x,t) = 0Δ(x,t)=0, the PDE is parabolic in (x,t)(x,t)(x,t)

(iii) Δ(x,t)<0\Delta(x,t) < 0Δ(x,t)<0, the PDE is hyperbolic in (x,t)(x,t)(x,t)

These definitions are motivated by the definition of conic sections. It is interesting to note, that the type of the PDE has strong implications about the solution behaviour. Hence, there are typical processes that are in nature either elliptic (e.g. gravity), hyperbolic (wave equations, advection), or parabolic (heat equation):

If we consider again our simple 1D constant coefficient heat equation and compute its type, we get Δ(x,t)=0, ∀(x,t)\Delta(x,t) = 0,\;\forall (x,t)Δ(x,t)=0,∀(x,t). Hence, the heat equation is a parabolic PDE.

In general, parabolic PDEs model processes that evolve in time and are not reversible in time, such as friction, diffusion, dissipation, etc. The effect over time is a "smearing" of the quantity, smoothing out of large gradients and extrema.
